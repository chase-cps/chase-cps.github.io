Contract-based verification#

The verification tasks on contracts ask whether one contract refines another, whether a contract can be met at all, and whether a component or an environment satisfies a contract. They are defined on behaviors, for any assume-guarantee contracts. CHASE carries them out for contracts in LTL over Boolean, bounded integer, real, and word variables: each task reduces to the validity or the satisfiability of a formula, which a model checker decides (back ends).

Verification tasks#

Task Holds when Python
refinement C1 ⪯ C2 A2 → A1 and (A1→G1) → (A2→G2) are valid refines(c1, c2)
compatibility some environment satisfies A is_compatible(c)
consistency some behavior satisfies A → G is_consistent(c)
assumptions and guarantees together A ∧ G is satisfiable (so compatible and consistent) verify(c)
implementation M ⊨M C M ∧ A → G is valid, or every behavior of the state machine M satisfies A → G is_implementation(m, c)
environment E ⊨E C E → A is valid, or every behavior of E satisfies A is_environment(e, c)
receptiveness of a state machine M M refuses no input that the assumptions allow is_receptive(m, c)
no redundant terms each term is not implied by the others remove_redundant(c)

M and E are LTL formulas over the variables of the contract. A failed check comes with a counterexample: a behavior that breaks it.

remove_redundant(c) drops the terms implied by the others, keeping A → G; before synthesis, use remove_redundant(c, synth=True) (GR(1) synthesis). With core=True, each removed term comes with the terms that imply it.

r = refines(impl, spec)
r.status        # "valid" or "invalid"
r.failed        # "assumptions" or "guarantees", when invalid
r.guarantees.trace

Queries on formulas#

The checks above are built on two queries, which can also be called directly:

Query Question Answer
implies(phi, psi, variables) validity: does phi → psi hold for every behavior? valid, or invalid with a behavior that satisfies phi and violates psi
is_satisfiable(phi, variables) satisfiability: does some behavior satisfy phi? sat with such a behavior, or unsat

A formula is satisfiable; a component satisfies a contract

is_satisfiable asks whether some behavior (a value of each variable at each step) satisfies a formula. Whether a component satisfies a contract is is_implementation(m, c).

Cores: why a check fails#

When the terms of a contract cannot hold together, unsat_core gives a minimal set of them that cannot: remove any one, and the others can.

from chase_cps import Contract, ltl, unsat_core, verify

bad = Contract.from_strings(inputs=["req"], outputs=["grant"], name="Bad",
                            guarantees=["G (req -> X grant)", "G !grant", "F req"])
print(verify(bad).status)
print([ltl.to_str(g) for g in unsat_core(bad).core.guarantees])
unsat
['G (req -> X grant)', 'G !grant', 'F req']

What the core says depends on what is in it:

  • only assumptions: the contract is not compatible;
  • only guarantees: the guarantees contradict each other;
  • both: the guarantees cannot hold when the assumptions do.

is_compatible(c, core=True), is_consistent(c, core=True), and is_satisfiable(phi, variables, core=True) return the core with a failed result.

Context#

Some formulas are neither an assumption nor a guarantee: they say what any behavior is like. Examples are the axiom of an encoding of finite traces, a physical law, or "one tool call at each step". Pass them as context, and the checks only look at the behaviors that satisfy them.

Example. Finite traces are often encoded with a variable alive, true for the length of the trace and false after it; the axiom alive & (alive U G !alive) says that a trace starts and ends. These two guarantees contradict each other on every finite trace:

FINITE = "alive & (alive U G !alive)"
c = Contract.from_strings(inputs=["alive"], outputs=["a"], name="C",
                          guarantees=["G (alive -> a)", "G (alive -> !a)"])
verify(c).status                     # sat
verify(c, context=FINITE).status     # unsat
is_consistent(c.assume(FINITE)).status   # sat

Without the context, the behavior where alive is false from the start satisfies both guarantees. With the context, that behavior is left out.

A context is not an assumption

As an assumption the axiom does not help (the last line): a behavior that violates an assumption satisfies the contract, since A → G holds. A behavior that violates the context does not count at all.

All the checks on this page take a context; the GR(1) synthesis checks do not.

Real variables#

A real variable takes any real value. When the reals are only compared with constants, or with each other, the checks are exact and work for synthesis too: each real is replaced by the interval between two constants it falls in. Otherwise nuXmv decides them with its SMT engine, exact for linear arithmetic. The result says which in reals. The Lift-Plus-Cruise demo uses real variables.

Words (bit vectors) and past(x, n) go to nuXmv as they are; the values of words in the traces are integers.

RTL#

A Verilog or SystemVerilog module is checked as a state machine, read with Yosys: see Hardware: SVA and RTL.

Results#

The checks of every behavior (implies, refines, is_implementation, is_environment) answer valid or invalid; those of some behavior (is_satisfiable, is_compatible, is_consistent, verify) answer sat or unsat; is_receptive answers receptive or not_receptive. Any check may answer unknown, with a reason (a time out, no tool installed).

A trace is a list of states, prefix then loop, the loop repeated forever. to_dict() gives the result as JSON.

Formulas over a bounded number of steps#

A formula with no G, F, U, R reads a bounded number of steps (X, x', past(x, n), and intervals such as G[0,10] p). A contract made of formulas of one step is a static contract: a budget, a sizing, a choice of parts. With z3 installed (pip install chase-cps[z3]), such formulas are checked by z3 instead of nuXmv, exactly for linear arithmetic over integers and reals (SMT and optimization).

minimize(objective, constraints) and maximize(objective, constraints) give the best value of a term where the constraints hold, and the values that reach it:

from chase_cps import Contract, maximize, minimize

gen = Contract.from_strings(name="Gen", inputs={"n": (0, 20)}, outputs={"p": "real"},
                            assumptions=["n <= 4"], guarantees=["p >= 2.5 * n", "p <= 30"])
print(maximize("n", gen).value)            # 4
print(minimize("p - 2.5 * n", gen).value)  # 0: the guarantee p >= 2.5 n is tight

The status is optimal, not_attained (the bound of a strict inequality), unbounded, infeasible, or unknown. When the objective is a formula, its robustness (below) is minimized: its worst case under the constraints.

Robustness#

The robustness of a formula on a trace says how much the trace satisfies it (a positive number) or violates it (a negative number), in the unit of the terms. It is the quantitative semantics of signal temporal logic, in discrete time, on the traces of the checks:

  • a comparison is the distance to its boundary: x <= 30 is 30 - x, x >= 10 is x - 10, x = c is -|x - c|, and x != c is |x - c|;
  • ! is the opposite, & the minimum, | the maximum;
  • X is the value at the next step, G the minimum over the steps to come, F the maximum, and U the maximum over the steps j of the right side at j, bounded by the left side before j.

0 is the boundary: the formula may hold or not (holds says which). The robustness is useful for formulas that compare numbers; a Boolean is inf or -inf.

from chase_cps.queries import Trace
from chase_cps.robustness import robustness

trace = Trace(prefix=[{"p": 20}], loop=[{"p": 27.5}, {"p": 25}])  # 20, then 27.5, 25, ...
robustness("G (p <= 30)", trace)          # 2.5: p stays 2.5 below 30
robustness("F[0,1] (p >= 27.5)", trace)   # 0: reached, on the boundary

A check that fails gives the terms that its counterexample violates (violated in the result), each with its robustness: a guarantee G (p >= 12) violated with -4 is missed by 4 at some step. These are the terms broken by this counterexample; others may fail on other behaviors.

Sampled signals: from steps to continuous time#

When a step is a sample of a continuous signal, taken every period seconds, and each variable changes at most its Lipschitz constant per second, chase_cps.sampled gives a stricter formula to check at the samples: if it holds there, the formula holds at every instant (Fainekos and Pappas, TCS 2009, Corollary 44). Each comparison gets a margin, and the intervals are adjusted by one step.

from chase_cps.sampled import sampled

print(sampled("G[0,8] (x <= 11)", {"x": 1}, 0.5))     # G[0,9] (x + 0.5 < 11)

With a period, intervals may be written with times (G[0s,2s] p). The command line checks a refinement in continuous time:

$> chase-cps check plant.ltl --period 1 --lipschitz x=1 --refines Plant Req

The margins are a sufficient condition: the answer is valid (it holds between the samples too) or unknown, never invalid; the same check without --lipschitz says whether it fails at the samples already. A term that links two samples (x' = x + u, the model of a plant in steps) stays as it is. The Room thermostat demo uses it.
