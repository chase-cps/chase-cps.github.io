GR(1) synthesis#

The algebra and the verification tasks hold for any contracts. This page is about contracts in the GR(1) fragment of LTL, for which a component can be synthesized. The analyses ask whether a component can be synthesized from a contract: a controller that sets the outputs so that the guarantees hold against every environment that sets the inputs and meets the assumptions. This depends on the synthesis approach, here GR(1) with slugs; it is not a property of contracts in general.

The examples below are from the Charger demo: two robots ask for a charging station (req1, req2), and an arbiter gives it (grant1, grant2).

The GR(1) form#

Each assumption and guarantee must be one of:

Kind Form Example
initial no temporal operator !grant1
safety G f, with only X (or x') in f G !(grant1 & grant2)
justice G F f G F req1

In addition, the environment cannot read the next value of the outputs: the initial assumptions read the inputs only, and the safety assumptions read only the next value of the inputs. A contract that is not in this form gives unknown, with the term at fault.

Realizability and synthesis#

Python Answer
is_realizable(c) realizable, or unrealizable with the counter-strategy of the environment
synthesize(c) the same, and the controller (a state machine) when realizable

Example. The arbiter Greedy gives the station to a robot at the next step after its request, and never to both:

G (req1 -> X grant1)     R6.1
G (req2 -> X grant2)     R6.2
G !(grant1 & grant2)     R5

When both robots ask at once, it cannot keep its promise: Greedy is unrealizable. The arbiter Fair promises a grant eventually instead, G F (grant1 | !req1), and is realizable:

cs = load_spec("Charger/charger.ltl")
is_realizable(cs["Greedy"]).status      # unrealizable
r = synthesize(cs["Fair"])              # realizable, a controller with 18 states

Writing the specification for a GR(1) solver#

slugs is the solver that CHASE runs. To run a solver yourself, to_gr1(c, format=...) writes the contract as its input:

  • format="slugs" (the default): the file that chase-cps gives to slugs.
  • format="gr1c": the input of gr1c.
open("fair.structuredSlugs", "w").write(to_gr1(cs["Fair"]))
open("fair.spc", "w").write(to_gr1(cs["Fair"], format="gr1c"))
# gr1c -r fair.spc      realizability

The inputs of the contract are the variables of the environment, and its outputs are those of the system. gr1c compares an integer only with a constant. Thus, for gr1c, a relation with arithmetic or between variables (x' = x + 1) is written as the values that satisfy it.

Unrealizable cores#

An unrealizable contract can have many guarantees. unrealizable_core (or is_realizable(c, core=True)) gives a minimal set of guarantees that is unrealizable under all the assumptions: remove any one, and the rest is realizable.

r = is_realizable(cs["Greedy"], core=True)
r.core.core.guarantees
G (req1 -> X grant1)
G (req2 -> X grant2)
G !(grant1 & grant2)

The two initial guarantees of Greedy (!grant1, !grant2) are not in the core: they play no part in the conflict. The search follows QuickCore (Maoz and Shalom, ICSE 2021).

Well-separation#

A contract is realizable for a bad reason when the controller can win by making the environment break its assumptions. Such a controller satisfies the contract (A → G holds when A fails) without doing its job.

Example. The arbiter Pushy assumes two things about robot 1:

G F req1                     A2   robot 1 asks infinitely often
G (grant1 -> X !req1)        A3   robot 1 stops asking after a grant

If the arbiter gives grant1 at every step, A3 makes robot 1 stop asking from the next step on, and A2 can then never hold. The arbiter wins without serving anybody. is_well_separated finds it:

r = is_well_separated(cs["Pushy"], core=True)
r.status                     # not_separated
r.core.core.assumptions      # A2 and A3
r.controller                 # the strategy that forces the violation
is_well_separated(cs["Polite"]).status     # separated

Polite assumes G ((req1 & grant1) -> X !req1) instead of A3: robot 1 stops asking only after a grant it asked for. The arbiter can no longer force a violation, and the contract is well-separated.

The check asks whether the controller can still win with the goal G F false (Klein and Pnueli, HVC 2010; Maoz and Ringert, FSE 2016): if it can, it wins only by breaking the assumptions, while keeping its own initial and safety guarantees.

Vacuity#

A contract is vacuous when the controller can win without an output that a liveness guarantee asks for: the assumptions already make the guarantee hold, and it obliges nothing. is_vacuous strengthens the guarantees and asks slugs whether the contract is still realizable (Bloem, Chockler, Ebrahimi, and Strichman, FMSD 2021):

Mode Query, for an output y Meaning
never A, G + a winning strategy never sets y
eventually A, G + {!p, G (p → X p), G (p → !y), G F p}, p a new output a winning strategy stops setting y from some step on

The outputs checked are those that a justice guarantee asks to set (they occur only positively in it).

Example. Fair is not vacuous: it cannot win without granting each robot. An arbiter that trusts the robots to stop asking on their own is:

fair = cs["Fair"]
trusting = Contract("Trusting", fair.inputs, fair.outputs,
                    [ltl.parse("G F !req1"), ltl.parse("G F !req2")], fair.guarantees)
is_vacuous(fair).status                  # not_vacuous
r = is_vacuous(trusting, core=True)
for v in r.vacuous:
    print(v.output, v.mode, [ltl.to_str(a) for a in v.core.core])
grant1 never ['G F !req1']
grant2 never ['G F !req2']

With G F !req1, the guarantee G F (grant1 | !req1) holds without any grant. The core is the assumption that makes it so; it is too strong.

Redundancy in the GR(1) game#

A term is redundant in the game when removing it does not change the synthesis problem: the same contracts are realizable, with the same controllers (the inherent vacuity of Maoz and Shalom, ESEC/FSE 2020). remove_redundant(c, synth=True) removes these terms.

Example. Fair has these justice guarantees:

G F (grant1 | !req1)             R7.1
G F (grant2 | !req2)             R7.2
G F (grant1 | !req1 | grant2)    R8

R8 is weaker than R7.1, so it adds nothing:

v = remove_redundant(cs["Fair"], synth=True, core=True)
for t in v.removed:
    print(t.part, t.kind, ltl.to_str(t.term), [ltl.to_str(x) for x in t.core])
guarantee justice G F (grant1 | !req1 | grant2) ['G F (grant1 | !req1)']

Without synth, remove_redundant (contract-based verification) keeps A → G instead. The two differ only when the assumptions are wrong: in the game, the component must keep its safety guarantees even when the environment breaks its justice assumptions. Use synth=True before synthesis, and the default before the algebra and the checks.
