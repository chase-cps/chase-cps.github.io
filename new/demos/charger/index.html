Robot charging station: refinement does not see the conflict, realizability does#

The system. Two robots share one charging station, and an arbiter decides who charges. This is a teaching example, made for these pages; its requirements are numbered in requirements.txt. Robot i asks for the station (req_i) when its battery is low (low_i), and assumes that it gets it (grant_i), or stops asking, infinitely often. The arbiter reads the requests and sets the grants; it must never give the station to both robots, G !(grant1 & grant2). The contracts are in LTL over Booleans, in the GR(1) form of the synthesis tool slugs, so CHASE can ask whether a controller can be built from a contract (realizable) and build it. ChargingStation is the contract of the whole system.

The question. Five candidate arbiters are written: Greedy, Fair, Pushy, Polite, and Trusting. Four of them are flawed in a different way. Which check catches which flaw, and which arbiter works with the robots?

The system: an arbiter (here Fair) between the two robots, inside ChargingStation.

The steps#

Step 1. Greedy: unrealizable, yet it refines. Greedy grants each request at the next step, G (req1 -> X grant1) and the same for robot 2. When both robots ask at once it cannot serve both without breaking G !(grant1 & grant2): it is unrealizable, no arbiter can do it. The unrealizable core, the smallest set of guarantees in conflict, is these three formulas. Yet Greedy with the robots refines ChargingStation: refinement only compares formulas (every behavior allowed by the parts is allowed by the system) and does not ask whether a part can exist.

Step 1: the core of Greedy in red.

Step 2. Fair: realizable. Fair gives the station to each robot, or the robot stops asking, infinitely often, G F (grant1 | !req1) and the same for robot 2. It is realizable, and slugs builds a controller of 18 states. It is not vacuous: it cannot meet its guarantees without setting its outputs (¬V). remove_redundant removes its last guarantee, G F (grant1 | !req1 | grant2), implied by the first one.

Step 2: Fair is realizable (R) and not vacuous (¬V).

Step 3. Realizable for a bad reason. Pushy assumes that robot 1 asks infinitely often, G F req1, but never right after a grant, G (grant1 -> X !req1). By granting robot 1 at every step, the arbiter makes the robot break the first assumption, and then owes nothing. A contract is well separated (WS) when the component cannot win this way; Pushy is not, and the core is the two assumptions. Polite, which assumes G ((req1 & grant1) -> X !req1) instead, is well separated.

Step 3: Pushy is not well separated; Polite is.

Step 4. An arbiter that never grants. Trusting is Fair with two more assumptions, G F !req1 and G F !req2: each robot stops asking infinitely often. Its guarantees then hold with no grant at all: it is vacuous. The core is the two assumptions and the guarantees they make true.

Step 4: Trusting is vacuous (¬V red).

Step 5. The arbiter with the robots. Fair and the robots feed each other. Composed with discharge (an assumption of one part that another part guarantees is dropped), they leave no assumption, and refine ChargingStation.

Step 5: the composition refines ChargingStation.

Each check of steps 1 to 4 is one call; by hand, each flaw means reasoning about every strategy of the robots. The checks are in GR(1) synthesis, the tags of the pictures in Diagrams.

pip install chase-cps
chase-cps install nuxmv --accept-license
chase-cps install slugs
python charger.py --expect
